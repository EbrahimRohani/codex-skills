# Working Backwards practical playbook

Source: `Working_Backwards_Practical_Playbook.pdf` and the matching completed handout.

## One-sentence method

Start with a specific customer outcome, write the future experience before the mechanism, expose the hardest unknowns, and make the next decision explicit.

## When to use it

Use the full method when the customer problem, success signal, or boundary is ambiguous; product, technology, operations, policy, or quality must make one promise; the decision creates meaningful customer exposure or hard-to-reverse investment; or important assumptions remain untested. Use a lighter outcome and decision record for routine, low-risk, reversible changes with clear evidence and ownership.

## Six-step chain

| Step | Question | Finished output |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Frame customer and outcome | Who is in one specific situation, what is the struggle, and what observable result matters? | Customer / struggle / outcome statement |
| 2. Write future promise | What will the headline, benefit, experience, success signal, boundary, and fallback be if this exists? | Future press release and experience narrative |
| 3. Ask hard FAQs | What could fail across recovery, accessibility, feasibility, architecture, operations, quality, safety, abuse, policy, data, and economics? | FAQs with guardrails and refusal conditions |
| 4. Label uncertainty | What is evidence, assumption, experiment, or decision, and how bounded is each? | Evidence and assumption ledger |
| 5. Recommend next decision | Should the team test, invest, pause, or stop, and what evidence changes that choice? | Bounded recommendation and staged evidence plan |
| 6. Set decision rights | Who integrates the work, who supplies evidence, who ratifies exposure, and when is the checkpoint? | Written decision record |

Repeat an earlier step whenever evidence weakens the promise or a hard FAQ changes the boundary. Keep the promise first and the mechanism second.

## Decision-ready quality gates

1. Promise: customer, struggle, benefit, and outcome cohere; the experience covers request, confirmation, fulfillment or handoff, status, and recovery; the mechanism remains subordinate.
2. Evidence: observations are sourced, dated, bounded, and limited; targets are labeled as targets; every high-impact assumption has a credible learning method and owner.
3. Boundary and guardrails: scope protects the learning question; fallback, accessibility, quality, safety, privacy, policy, and economic controls are explicit; entry and stop criteria have named authority.
4. Ownership and finish line: one DRI integrates input; a sponsor ratifies hard-to-reverse exposure or investment; decision, dissent, remaining risk, next action, deadline, and review checkpoint are recorded.

## Roles

Accountability stays singular even when input is broad. Typical lenses are Product or initiative DRI, Design, Engineering Manager, Tech Lead, Engineers, QA, Data, Operations or service owners, Risk / policy / legal, and a ratifying sponsor (sometimes called the Battle-Owner). Specialists own evidence in their domains; the DRI integrates it and owns the result.

## Common failure modes

- Mechanism first: architecture or process is proposed before the outcome and experience.
- Customer too broad: generic segments hide eligibility, need, and recovery differences.
- Target presented as evidence: ambition is mistaken for a baseline or validated threshold.
- FAQs as reassurance: answers defend the concept instead of exposing reasons to narrow or refuse.
- Consensus replaces ownership: many contributors, no integrating DRI.
- Exposure expands too quickly: a live promise starts before cheaper evidence.
- No written stop rule: the team can start but cannot say who pauses, when, or why.

Carry four habits: customer first, future promise, evidence, and ownership.
